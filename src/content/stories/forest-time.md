The first thing I noticed was how quiet we became. A few minutes beyond the
trailhead, the conversation thinned out. Our footsteps settled into the soft
rhythm of the forest floor. Somewhere above us, a branch creaked.

## A different kind of clock

At the measurement plot, we wrapped a tape around the same trees that students
had measured years before us. Each number belonged to a much longer
conversation. A season of growth might fit inside a few millimetres; a lifetime
of weather remained folded into the wood.

> A forest does not ask us to slow down. It simply offers another pace.

## What the measurements leave out

The notebook records diameter, height, and species. It has no column for the
smell of damp cedar, or for the moment when sunlight reaches a patch of moss.
These observations matter too. They remind me that learning about a place
begins with being present in it.

## Taking the quiet home

Back in the city, I caught myself walking more slowly. I stopped beside a
street tree I had passed a hundred times. Its roots had lifted a small square of
pavement. The forest had followed me home, in the form of a question: what else
have I been walking past?
